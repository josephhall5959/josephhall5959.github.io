\section{History of Financial Technology}
%--------------------------------------------------------------------

\begin{frame}{What is ``Financial Technology''?}

In this class, ``Financial Technologies'' are physical and social processes that determine the limitations of the two financial prerequisites for modern business: 

\begin{itemize}
    \item Money: How value can be stored, denominated, and transferred
    \item Credit: How investment contracts can be written and enforced
\end{itemize}

Naturally, limitations change over time with technological breakthroughs and changes in social institutions
    
\end{frame}



\subsection{What is Money?}
%------------------------------------------------

\begin{frame}{What Is Money?}

    Money fulfills three roles in an economy: 
    \begin{itemize}
        \item A Store of Value \textcolor{red}{(Implies hard to counterfeit)}
        \item A Medium of Exchange \textcolor{red}{(Ownership can change)} 
        \item A Unit of Account \textcolor{red}{(Units to list prices)}
    \end{itemize}
    What ingredients are \textbf{necessary} to enable something to fulfill these roles? 
    
        \vfill \pause 
        
    \begin{center}
            \huge Scarcity + Coordination = Money 
    \end{center}
\vfill 
    Any specific form of money is called a \textbf{currency}
    
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What Problem does Money Solve?}

\pause 
Money solves the \textbf{``double coincidence of wants''} problem (what does that mean?) \pause 

\begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width = .8\textwidth]{dcow.png}
\end{center}

Without money, trade requires that I want what you have, \textbf{and} that you have what I want (a ``double coincidence of wants'') 
   
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{Enforcing Scarcity: Specie}

It is obvious to use as money something that is naturally scarce, called \textbf{specie}, historically gold or silver 
\bi 
\m \textbf{Coinage}, shaping specie into standardized denominations, made money much more convenient to use 
\m Coinage is vulnerable to \textbf{Clipping}: the practice of shaving small amounts of metal off the edges of coins \pause 
\m Sir Isaac Newton was appointed to Warden of the Royal Mint in 1696 and invented the practice of ridged indents into the sides of coins to prevent clipping  
\ei 
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{Enforcing Scarcity: Minting}


\textbf{Minting}: Monopolizing a minting technique which is hard to counterfeit 

\bi 
\m Creates \textbf{seignorage} revenue for the mint owner \pause  
\m Non-counterfeitability must be easily \textbf{verifiable!} \pause 
\m Modern governments rely on \textbf{fiat} to enforce coordination when minting paper money \pause 
\m The US Secret Service was originally created as an anti-counterfeiting unit 
\m \textbf{Cryptography} is a verifiable anti-counterfeiting technology using \textbf{no specie} and backed by \textbf{no fiat} -- scarcity and coordination are all you need!
\ei 



\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Desirable features of a currency:}

\bi 
\m \textbf{Convenience}: Easy to transfer 
\bi
\m The Mongols used paper money as early as 1227, 701 years before the United States did. Transaction costs are sensitive to weight when you run the world's largest land empire and coordinate using horses \pause 
\m \textbf{Standard denominations} abrogate the need for measurement scales for every transaction \pause 
\ei
\m \textbf{Price Stability}
\bi 
\m Entrepreneurship is safer when future prices are known today \pause 
\m Savings are not eroded too fast \pause 
\m Fiat currency (printed by a government, enforced only by law) vulnerable to inflation if policymakers abuse \emph{seignorage}, the profit from owning the money printer 
\ei 
\m \textbf{Fungibility}: Any unit of currency is identical to any other \pause 
\bi 
\m Helps with use as \emph{unit of account}
\m ``Bi-metallic standard'' was used historically but proved unworkable
\ei 
\ei
\end{frame}



\begin{frame}{Price Stability}

\textbf{Price Stability} requires that the money supply grow \emph{in proportion to the economy}

\pause 

\textbf{The Quantity Identity of the Price Level: }

$$MV = PY$$

Where: 
\begin{itemize}
    \item $M =$ The money supply 
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Bitcoin has a \emph{strict cap of 21 million} \pause 
        \item Ethereum actively manages the \emph{growth rate} of ETH with the goal of price stability \pause
        \pause 
    \end{itemize}
    \item $V =$ Monetary velocity: avg \# of times a unit of currency changes hands per year
    \begin{itemize}
        \item This can be affected by, e.g., fractional reserve banking, where savings are lent out \pause 
    \end{itemize}
    \item $P =$ The price level: the price of a constant basket of goods 
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Change in price level = inflation \pause 
    \end{itemize}
    \item $Y =$ Real economic activity (that occurs in said currency)\pause 
    \begin{itemize}
        \item ``Real'' meaning measured in units of real goods, not measured in currency units 
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
    
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{MV = PY}

Recall the identity: 
$$MV = PY$$

\pause 


\begin{itemize}
    \item Fixing $M$ and $V$ with rising $Y$ implies \emph{falling} P: ``deflation''
    \item FDR suspended the convertibility of U.S. dollars for Gold in 1933 after four years of deflation \pause 
    \item Deflation is bad because it disincentivizes investment and spending: money is hoarded instead of being invested and spent: monetary velocity falls further, creating a vicious spiral \pause 
    \item Modern monetary policy is to maintain a 2\% rate of inflation: just enough so that investors need to put money into bonds and stocks instead of vaults 
    \item With positive inflation, inefficient industries can slowly shrink in real terms without giving workers a nominal pay cut 
\end{itemize}
    
\end{frame}

\subsection{What is Credit?}
%------------------------------------------------

\begin{frame}{What is Credit?}

Credit is an investment contract. It can take many forms: 
\begin{itemize}
   \item A bond, invented in ancient Greece and popularized by medieval European kings to fight wars (See, e.g., \hyperlink{https://sites.google.com/view/matthijskorevaar/research?authuser=0}{Matthijs Korevar}) \pause 
    \item Shares of a joint stock company (invented in England in 1551, popularized by the Dutch) \pause 
    
\end{itemize}
Forms of credit can become money-like if they are \emph{safe}
    \bi 
    \m Modern banks keep their money as Federal Bank Reserves, a form of credit 
    \m Credit has a hard time being \emph{fungible}: it is \textbf{informationally-sensitive} by design, in the sense of \hyperlink{https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2552018}{Holmstr\"om}
    \bi
    \m The price of credit is a market signal of the price of risk and time. Money is meant to be insensitive to such things. 
    \ei
    \ei 
    
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What is an ``Investment Contract''}


\textbf{Question:} What is the SEC? 

\pause

The \textbf{Securities Exchange Act of 1934} gave the S.E.C., the \textbf{Securities Exchange Commission} authority to regulate ``investment contracts''. 
\vfill 

\noindent The Supreme Court in 1946 created the \emph{Howey} test to define the term: 

\begin{quote}
    ``An `investment contract' exists when there is the investment of money in a common enterprise with a reasonable expectation of profits to be derived from the efforts of others''
\end{quote}
\pause \vfill 
In other words, credit is ``Man's Confidence in Man'' 

\end{frame}
